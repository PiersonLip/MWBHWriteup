01 · Motivation

We're about to find a lot more black holes

On paper, the Milky Way should have hundreds of millions of stellar-mass black holes. Almost none of them glow. Until recently we mostly knew the loud ones sitting in X-ray binaries (a few dozen with solid mass measurements), plus whatever LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA finds far away.

That's changing fast. Gaia already confirmed three sleepy black holes with bright companions (Gaia BH1, BH2, BH3). Roman should microlens hundreds more. Gaia DR4 is expected to add dozens of astrometric BH binaries. So the fun question is shifting from "find me one cool system" to "what does the whole population look like?"

You can't answer that by just counting corpses. Most massive stars grow up with a roommate. Stuff like mass transfer, common envelopes, mergers, and supernova kicks decide whether a black hole ends up alone or paired, heavy or light, tight or wide. A lot of older population codes approximate that with fitting formulae. This paper uses POSYDON instead, which leans on detailed MESA binary grids.

The basic idea: take today's Galactic black holes, trace them back to how they formed, poke the uncertain physics to see what sticks, then ask (at a first-pass level) what Gaia and Roman might actually notice.

02 · Methods

Fancy binaries, simple Galaxy

POSYDON evolves single stars and binaries from the ZAMS using pre-computed MESA grids. The main ("fiducial") run throws 10 million binaries at solar metallicity, then scales the results up to a Milky Way-ish galaxy (~6×10¹⁰ M☉ of stars, steady star formation for 10 Gyr). "Today" means: anything that still has a black hole in it at 10 Gyr.

Some stuff stays locked: a Kroupa-like IMF for the primary stars, POSYDON's wind and mixing setup, and the usual αCE-λCE common envelope recipe. Across twelve models, they flip the knobs that actually rearrange the BH census:

Initial binaries Moe & Di Stefano (2017) period/mass-ratio/eccentricity correlations, versus a simpler Sana+12 + flat-q setup.
Core collapse Fryer delayed engine as the default, plus Sukhbold+16 and Patton+20 options that can still make low-mass BHs.
Natal kicks Disberg log-normal NS kicks, then scaled for BHs by mass, fallback, full NS-like, or zero. There's also a Hobbs Maxwellian version.
Common-envelope efficiency αCE = 1.0 (default), 0.5, and 0.1.
Metallicity Z☉, 0.1 Z☉, and 0.01 Z☉. Winds (and that ~13 M☉ bump) care a lot about this.

When they want to talk about observations, they drop the synthetic systems into a Milky Way model and integrate orbits with gala, updating velocities whenever a compact object is born. Full survey selection effects are left for later. This paper is about the intrinsic population, plus a first look at where that population sits in observable space.

03 · Demographics

Most Galactic black holes are alone (but they usually weren't born that way)

In the fiducial solar-metallicity run, you get about 4×10⁸ stellar-mass black holes in the Galaxy. Roughly three-quarters are single today. The rest still have a partner. That loneliness is usually earned: stellar mergers and kicked-apart binaries beat out stars that were born alone.

For the loners, stellar mergers win (42% of all BHs): two normal stars smash together (often after a failed common envelope), then the merger product later collapses. Disrupted binaries come next (27%): a natal kick and/or sudden mass loss rips the binary apart at the first or second core collapse. Born-single stars, BH+star mergers, and rare DCO mergers fill out the leftovers.

The binaries that survive look lopsided. Detached BH-BH systems alone are ~19% of all BHs. BH+NS, BH+WD, and BH+star systems together are only a few percent. Funny enough, those rare ones are basically everything we've observed so far.

The mass spectra keep showing two fingerprints: a low-mass bump near ~2.5 to 5 M☉ (helped by stable mass transfer before BH formation, plus the delayed explosion engine) and a skinny pile-up near ~13 M☉ from windy Wolf-Rayet evolution. Median masses land around ~8.2 M☉ (isolated) and ~12.5 M☉ (in binaries).

04 · Natal kicks & disruption

A kick decides who stays together

When a star goes boom, two things mess with the orbit. Symmetric mass loss shifts the center of mass (the Blaauw effect). Asymmetries can also give the remnant an extra natal kick. Add those up and you get the post-supernova orbital energy. If that energy is positive, the binary is done. Everyone flies free.

Neutron star kicks are somewhat pinned down by pulsar motions. The fiducial model draws from the Disberg et al. log-normal (peak around 150 to 200 km/s). Black hole kicks? Much mushier. The paper's default assumes the same kick momentum as for neutron stars, so heavier remnants get slower kicks:

vBH = vNS · MNS / MBH   ·   fallback:  vkick = (1 − ffb) · vNS

The extremes are useful bookends. Full NS-like kicks smash most binaries apart. Zero BH kicks keep almost everyone bound and let detached systems stretch out. Fallback-reduced kicks are kinder to massive direct-collapse remnants, which helps the ~13 M☉ wind feature show up among survivors.

Disruption is a big reason so many BHs are single, and why low-mass BHs are especially easy to unbind (they get the bigger kicks under inverse-mass scaling). Play with it below. Widen the orbit, crank the kick, or flip to NS-like kicks and watch the pair come undone.

05 · Formation pathways

Two roads to a BH-BH binary (and a ~13 M☉ fingerprint)

Detached BH-BH binaries are the most common surviving setup, and they still remember what happened during the star+star phase. Two channels stand out before the first black hole forms:

Stable mass transfer strips the primary, smears final BH masses over a wide range, and likes making lower-mass black holes. It's also the main path to the low-mass bumps you see in disrupted systems and a lot of BH+"other" binaries.

Wind-driven, non-interacting evolution takes over for very massive primaries (≳80 M☉) at solar metallicity. Strong winds keep the star from puffing up too much, so Roche-lobe overflow never happens. Then Wolf-Rayet winds shove a wide range of ZAMS masses toward a narrow final mass, and you get black holes clustered near ~13 M☉. That plateau is especially sharp among detached BH-BH systems.

Metallicity is the soft underbelly of that fingerprint. Lower Z weakens winds, washes out the plateau, makes BHs heavier, and lets stable mass transfer own the high-mass end. At 0.01 Z☉ a few systems even poke past the pair-instability gap.

Stellar mergers (the biggest isolated channel) also care about when mass transfer goes unstable. POSYDON doesn't lean on one magic critical mass ratio. Instability can come from crazy transfer rates, L2 overflow, contact with an evolved star, or photon-trapping around a compact accretor. Short-period contact, main-sequence CE, and evolved Roche-lobe overflow each feed different merger products, but post-merger winds still help build that ~13 M☉ feature.

06 · Common envelopes

αCE doesn't make black holes. It decides who survives.

Unstable mass transfer can dunk the binary into a common envelope. The efficiency αCE is the fraction of orbital energy used to blow that envelope off. Lower α means a deeper inspiral: more mergers, tighter survivors, and a harder time for unequal-mass systems.

In the paper's runs, dialing αCE from 1.0 to 0.5 to 0.1 boosts stellar mergers, BH+star mergers, and especially DCO mergers (BH-BH pulling ahead of BH-NS). Detached BH-BH counts tick up a bit, while BH+NS/WD/star detached systems drop. The BH mass peaks barely budge. CE rearranges who is bound to whom, not how heavy the remnants are.

07 · Galactic embedding

Drop the black holes into a real Galaxy

Binary evolution alone tells you what a system is. To talk about Gaia or Roman, you also need to know where it is. The paper takes POSYDON survivors and seeds them into a Milky Way model (low-α disk, high-α disk, central component), each with its own star-formation history and metallicity mix.

For every birth site they draw a formation time, position, and metallicity, then match it to the nearest POSYDON metallicity track. The system starts on a roughly circular orbit with a bit of random velocity scatter. From there, gala integrates the path forward in time.

Whenever POSYDON says a compact object forms, the Galactic velocity gets updated for mass loss and the natal kick (rotated from the binary frame into Galactocentric coordinates). If the binary stays bound, the pair keeps moving together. If it disrupts, the two pieces fly on their own from that moment on.

08 · Observational links

What Gaia and Roman can actually test

Gaia BH+star systems. Quiet black holes with luminous companions are rare in the full census, but they're gold for astrometry. POSYDON fills the middle ground between tight post-CE systems and wide non-interacting binaries with a solid stable mass transfer population. Rapid codes mostly miss that middle. Gaia BH1 and BH2 sit near it, which makes them look a little less like weirdos in mass-period space (matching every observed detail is still tough).

Roman microlensing of BH-BH lenses. After parking populations in a Galactic model and doing a simple Einstein-radius cut, about ~10% of bulge-pointed detached BH-BH systems land in the sweet spot for binary-lens signatures (0.7 < θsep/θ̃E < 1.8). If you can get component masses, the low-mass stable-MT pile-up and the ~13 M☉ wind feature become real observational tests.

These are intentionally rough links. Real survey forecasts need selection functions (gaiamock, PopSyCLE, and friends) on top of the kinematically evolved catalogs the paper already built.

Across core-collapse variants, the qualitative map holds: low-mass BH-BH ≈ stable mass transfer; narrow high-mass feature ≈ wind-driven non-interacting progenitors. Metallicity and winds decide where that feature sits, or whether it shows up at all.

09 · Conclusions

What to take home

Most Milky Way black holes are single today, but that usually means a binary story ended in a merger or a kick, not that the star was born alone. The binaries that stick around are mostly detached BH-BH systems with two solar-metallicity pathways baked in: stable mass transfer at lower masses, and a wind-driven ~13 M☉ plateau up high.

Natal kicks and common-envelope efficiency reshuffle who stays bound and how tight they are. Metallicity reshapes masses and can wipe out the wind fingerprint. Detailed binary evolution also turns up a stable-MT BH+star population that rapid codes underproduce, which is a nice place for Gaia to tell modeling approaches apart.

Next up in the paper's plan: forward-modeling. Take these intrinsic, kinematically evolved populations and ask what Gaia DR4 and Roman will actually detect.
