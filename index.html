01 · Motivation

We're about to find a whole lot more black holes

The Milky Way has hundreds of millions of stellar-mass1 black holes2. Yet almost none of them are visible. Until recently we were only aware of the bright ones sitting in X-ray binaries and the ones that are detected via gravitational waves by LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA.

However, that is rapidly changing. So far Gaia has already confirmed three quiet black holes with bright companions and Gaia DR4 is expected to have dozens more measured BH binaries, Roman should detect hundreds more isolated3 ones. Thus the question is quickly shifting from "can we find them?" to "how did they get there?"

And we can't answer that question just counting the remnants of stars. Most massive stars4 grow up intertwined with a partner in binary systems. These relationships are tumultuous, with things like mass transfer, common envelope, mergers, and supernova kicks deciding whether a black hole ends up alone or paired, massive or light, close-by or far away. Previous work approximates these processes; we use POSYDON instead, which lets us use much more accurate MESA simulations.

The big idea: model the black holes that we would see today, trace them back to how they formed, vary the physics to see what works best, then predict what Gaia and Roman might actually see.

  1. Whenever I say "stellar" it simply means "star-like." Here it means black holes that have masses that are on the same scale/order of magnitude as stars. ↩
  2. Order-of-magnitude census estimates put the number of stellar-mass black holes in the Milky Way around ~10⁸ (100 million). ↩
  3. I.e. They don't have a companion. ↩

02 · Methodology

Fancy binaries in a simplified Galaxy

In order to model all of these binaries in our galaxy, we use POSYDON, which evolves single stars and binaries from birth (ZAMS) all the way to their death over the scale of billions of years. We use a selection of simulations of 10 million binaries at varying metallicities1 and scale their results up to a Milky Way-like galaxy.

With these systems there is still a large amount of physics we don't fully understand, so we try variations and see what sticks best. The things that we tweak are:

Initial binary properties Masses, periods, mass ratios, and eccentricities drawn for the birth population.
Core collapse How the remnant mass is calculated when the iron core collapses.
Natal Kicks Kick strength, fallback amount, edge cases.
Common envelope efficiency How much drag is caused by the cores orbiting through the shared envolope.
Metallicity The Z grid that sets wind strength and which evolutionary paths are available.

03 · Milky Way demographics

Most Galactic black holes are alone (but they weren't born that way)

In our baseline solar-metallicity1 simulation, we found about 400,000,000 stellar-mass black holes in the Galaxy. Roughly three-quarters of these are single today, while the rest still have their binary comapnions.

For the formation of isolated black holes, stellar mergers are the most common (42% of all BHs): this happens stars drift closer and closer together until they merge into one (often after a failed common envelope), the merger product later collapsing into a black hole. Disrupted binaries come next (27%): these are black holes where a natal kick and/or sudden mass loss rips the binary apart, sending the black hole flying through our Milky Way. This can happen at the when either of the stars go supernova. Stars born single, BH+star mergers, and rare DCO mergers make up the rest.

The binary systems that survive are unique in their own right. Detached BH-BH systems alone are ~19% of all BHs. BH+NS, BH+WD, and BH+star systems together are only a few percent, yet this few percent are really the only ones we've observed so far.

04 · Natal kicks & disruption

The kick decides who stays together

When a star in a binary dies in the form of a supernova, two things mess with the orbit. The sudden extreme mass loss shifts the center of mass (referred to as the Blaauw effect or Blaauw kick) and any sort of asymmetries in the supernova can also give the remnant an extra natal kick1. If the combined effect of these is strong enough, the stars are ejected and the system becomes disrupted. This disruption from strong kicks is a big reason so many BHs are single, and why low-mass BHs are especially likely to be isolated2.

Parked · Formation pathways

Two roads to a BH-BH binary (and a ~13 M☉ fingerprint)

Detached BH-BH binaries are the most common surviving setup, and they still remember what happened during the star+star phase. Two channels stand out before the first black hole forms:

Stable mass transfer strips the primary, smears final BH masses over a wide range, and likes making lower-mass black holes. It's also the main path to the low-mass bumps you see in disrupted systems and a lot of BH+"other" binaries.

Wind-driven, non-interacting evolution takes over for very massive primaries (≳80 M☉) at solar metallicity. Strong winds keep the star from puffing up too much, so Roche-lobe overflow never happens. Then Wolf-Rayet winds shove a wide range of ZAMS masses toward a narrow final mass, and you get black holes clustered near ~13 M☉. That plateau is especially sharp among detached BH-BH systems.

Metallicity is the soft underbelly of that fingerprint. Lower Z weakens winds, washes out the plateau, makes BHs heavier, and lets stable mass transfer own the high-mass end. At 0.01 Z☉ a few systems even poke past the pair-instability gap.

Stellar mergers (the biggest isolated channel) also care about when mass transfer goes unstable. POSYDON doesn't lean on one magic critical mass ratio. Instability can come from crazy transfer rates, L2 overflow, contact with an evolved star, or photon-trapping around a compact accretor. Short-period contact, main-sequence CE, and evolved Roche-lobe overflow each feed different merger products, but post-merger winds still help build that ~13 M☉ feature.

Parked · Common envelopes

αCE doesn't make black holes. It decides who survives.

Unstable mass transfer can dunk the binary into a common envelope. The efficiency αCE is the fraction of orbital energy used to blow that envelope off. Lower α means a deeper inspiral: more mergers, tighter survivors, and a harder time for unequal-mass systems.

In the paper's runs, dialing αCE from 1.0 to 0.5 to 0.1 boosts stellar mergers, BH+star mergers, and especially DCO mergers (BH-BH pulling ahead of BH-NS). Detached BH-BH counts tick up a bit, while BH+NS/WD/star detached systems drop. The BH mass peaks barely budge. CE rearranges who is bound to whom, not how heavy the remnants are.

05 · Galactic embedding

Embedding the black holes into our Milky Way

POSYDON's simulations of binary evolution tell you what a system is. To actually talk about observations with Gaia or Roman, we need to know where it is. To do that, we take the systems from POSYDON and embed them into a mock Milky Way1.

What we do is first draw a birth location and time in the Milky Way for the binary. Based on this location and time, we can get an estimate of what the metallicity should be. From this metallicity, we find a suitable match from the POSYDON simulation. After we have our binary, we then put it on a roughly circular orbit around our Milky Way and use gala to integrate the orbit. Then, whenever a compact object forms2 in our POSYDON simulation, the system's velocity gets updated for mass loss kick and the natal kick.

06 · Observational properties

What we actually see

Quiet black holes with luminous companions1 are pretty rare in the full census, but they're extremtly useful when it comes to measuring black holes. This is because they're both much easier to spot due their bright companion, but also by measuring the effect the black hole has on its companion, we can get a exceptionally in depth understanding of the black holes properties.

Microlensing is a form of gravitational lensing where the brightness of a star increases dramitcally due to an object (in this case a black hole) passing infront and bending light around itself. This process is currently our only way of measuring isolated black hole2.

07 · Conclusions

The big ideas

While most black holes in our Milky Way are single today, our research suggests that this was not always the case, with the majority of them forming from mergers or disrutpion. The minority which remain in binaries are almost entirely in detached BH-BH systems.

Binary stars

Most of the stars you see in the night sky are not alone. They are typically binaries locked orbiting eachother.

What “binary” means

A binary system is simply two stars bound by gravity, orbiting a common shared center of mass. These stars can be nearly twins or compeltly different and their orbits can range on the scale of minutes to hundreds if not thousands of years. Some of these pairs you can see with a telescope (visual binaries). However, most only are only visibile due to a wobbling spectrum, eclipses, or unqiue light curves.

Unequal-mass binary · shared center of mass

“Binary” is also shorthand for the whole family of multiple-star systems. There is not reason why you can't have a close binary pair that has a wide teritary which orbits far away1, or have two binary pairs orbiting each other.

Way more common then you'd think

When people think of the iconic sunsets of tatooine they typically think it's some outlandish sci-fi concept, however, among low-mass stars like the Sun, around half have at least one companion and live in binaries. And when you start getting into more massive stars, that percentage climbs, quickly reaching nearly 100%.

Because of this relationship the bright night sky is binary-rich. The stars our eyes notice are often luminous, and luminous stars are disproportionately the ones that formed with partners.

Notes

  1. Note that despite the fact that this is three stars orbiting eachother, it noteably is not a three body problem. This is because all three stars are orbiting the same stable common center of mass, whereas in a three body problem there no such consistent center. ↩
