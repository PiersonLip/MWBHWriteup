Uncovering the physical origins of black holes in the Milky Way

A walkthrough of why a simulation tool like POSYDON can connect the physics of binary stars to the modern black holes of our galaxy, what Gaia and Roman might actually see, and why it matters.

01 · Motivation

We're about to find a whole lot more black holes

The Milky Way has hundreds of millions of stellar-mass1 black holes2. Yet almost none of them are visible. Until recently we were only aware of the bright ones sitting in X-ray binaries and the ones that are detected via gravitational waves by LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA.

However, that is rapidly changing. So far Gaia has already confirmed three quiet black holes with bright companions and Gaia DR4 is expected to have dozens more measured BH binaries. Furthmore, Roman should detect hundreds more isolated3 ones. Thus the question is quickly shifting from "can we find them?" to "how did they get here?."

And we can't answer that by just counting the corpses of stars. Most massive4 stars grow up intertwined with a partner in binary systems. These relationships are tumultuous, with things like mass transfer, common envelope, mergers, and supernova kicks deciding whether a black hole ends up alone or paired, massive or light, close-by or far away. A lot of simulation codes approximate these processes, however, we use POSYDON instead, which allows us to use much more accurate MESA simulations instead.

The basic idea: model the black holes that we would see today, trace them back to how they formed, vary the physics to see what works best, then predict Gaia and Roman might actually see.

02 · Methodology

Fancy binaries in a simplified Galaxy

In order to model all of these binaries in our galaxy, we use POSYDON, which evolves single stars and binaries from birth (ZAMS) all the way to their death over the scale of billions of years. We use a selection of simulations of 10 million binaries at varying metallicities1 and scale their results up to a Milky Way-like galaxy.

With these systems there is still a large amount of physics we don't fully understand, so we try variations and see what sticks best. However, a good chunk of the physics stays the same between simulations: we use a a Kroupa-like IMF for the primary stars, POSYDON's wind and mixing setup, and the usual common envelope with the standard process. 03 · Milky way demographics

Most Galactic black holes are alone (but they weren't born that way)

In our baseline solar-metallicity run, we found about 400,000,000 stellar-mass black holes in the Galaxy. Roughly three-quarters of these are single today, with the rest still having their binary partners.

For the solo stars, stellar mergers are the most common (42% of all BHs): two stars drift closer and closer together until they merge into one (often after a failed common envelope), the merger product later collapsing into a black hole. Disrupted binaries come next (27%): these are black holes where a natal kick and/or sudden mass loss rips the binary apart. This can happen at the first or second core collapse. Single born stars, BH+star mergers, and rare DCO mergers make up the rest.

The binary systems that survive look lopsided. Detached BH-BH systems alone are ~19% of all BHs. BH+NS, BH+WD, and BH+star systems together are only a few percent. Interestingly, this few percent are really the only ones we've observed so far.

The mass spectra keep showing two fingerprints: a low-mass bump near ~2.5 to 5 M☉ (helped by stable mass transfer before BH formation, plus the delayed explosion engine) and a skinny pile-up near ~13 M☉ from windy Wolf-Rayet evolution. Median masses land around ~8.2 M☉ (isolated) and ~12.5 M☉ (in binaries).

04 · Natal kicks & disruption

The kick decides who stays together

When a star in a binary dies in the form of a supernova, two things mess with the orbit. The sudden extreme mass loss shifts the center of mass (the Blaauw effect) and any sort of asymmetries can also give the remnant an extra natal kick1. If the combined effect of these is strong enough, the system becomes disrupted. This disruption from strong kicks is a big reason so many BHs are single, and why low-mass BHs are especially likely to be isolated2.

Parked · Formation pathways

Two roads to a BH-BH binary (and a ~13 M☉ fingerprint)

Detached BH-BH binaries are the most common surviving setup, and they still remember what happened during the star+star phase. Two channels stand out before the first black hole forms:

Stable mass transfer strips the primary, smears final BH masses over a wide range, and likes making lower-mass black holes. It's also the main path to the low-mass bumps you see in disrupted systems and a lot of BH+"other" binaries.

Wind-driven, non-interacting evolution takes over for very massive primaries (≳80 M☉) at solar metallicity. Strong winds keep the star from puffing up too much, so Roche-lobe overflow never happens. Then Wolf-Rayet winds shove a wide range of ZAMS masses toward a narrow final mass, and you get black holes clustered near ~13 M☉. That plateau is especially sharp among detached BH-BH systems.

Metallicity is the soft underbelly of that fingerprint. Lower Z weakens winds, washes out the plateau, makes BHs heavier, and lets stable mass transfer own the high-mass end. At 0.01 Z☉ a few systems even poke past the pair-instability gap.

Stellar mergers (the biggest isolated channel) also care about when mass transfer goes unstable. POSYDON doesn't lean on one magic critical mass ratio. Instability can come from crazy transfer rates, L2 overflow, contact with an evolved star, or photon-trapping around a compact accretor. Short-period contact, main-sequence CE, and evolved Roche-lobe overflow each feed different merger products, but post-merger winds still help build that ~13 M☉ feature.

Parked · Common envelopes

αCE doesn't make black holes. It decides who survives.

Unstable mass transfer can dunk the binary into a common envelope. The efficiency αCE is the fraction of orbital energy used to blow that envelope off. Lower α means a deeper inspiral: more mergers, tighter survivors, and a harder time for unequal-mass systems.

In the paper's runs, dialing αCE from 1.0 to 0.5 to 0.1 boosts stellar mergers, BH+star mergers, and especially DCO mergers (BH-BH pulling ahead of BH-NS). Detached BH-BH counts tick up a bit, while BH+NS/WD/star detached systems drop. The BH mass peaks barely budge. CE rearranges who is bound to whom, not how heavy the remnants are.

05 · Galactic embedding

Putting the black holes into our Milky Way

POSYDON's simulation of binary evolution tells you what a system is. To actually talk about observations with Gaia or Roman, we need to know where it is. To do that, we take the systems from POSYDON and put them into a mock Milky Way1.

What we do is first draw a birth location and time in the Milky Way for the binary. Based on this location and time, we can get an estimate of what the metallicity would be. From this metallicity, we find a suitable match from the POSYDON simulation. After we have our binary, we then put in on a roughly circular orbit around our Milky Way and use gala to integrate the orbit.

06 · Observational properties

What Gaia and Roman see

Quiet black holes with luminous companions are pretty rare in the full census, but they're incredibly useful to measuring the black holes. POSYDON fills the middle ground between tight post-CE systems and wide non-interacting binaries with a solid stable mass transfer population. Rapid codes mostly miss that middle. Gaia BH1 and BH2 sit near it, which makes them look a little less like weirdos in mass-period space (matching every observed detail is still tough).

Microlensing, briefly. A dark foreground mass — say a quiet black hole — can briefly bend and brighten light from a background bulge star. You usually can't resolve the warp. What you do see is a smooth brightening and fading: the light curve. Closer alignments (smaller impact parameter) make taller peaks. The green circle in the figure is the Einstein ring scale set by the lens mass and geometry; the two colored dots are the lensed images of the source.

Roman microlensing of BH-BH lenses. Roman's bulge survey is built for this kind of event — including lenses that are themselves binaries. After parking populations in a Galactic model and doing a simple Einstein-radius cut, about ~10% of bulge-pointed detached BH-BH systems land in the sweet spot for binary-lens signatures (0.7 < θsep/θ̃E < 1.8). If you can get component masses, the low-mass stable-MT pile-up and the ~13 M☉ wind feature become real observational tests.

These are intentionally rough links. Real survey forecasts need selection functions (gaiamock, PopSyCLE, and friends) on top of the kinematically evolved catalogs the paper already built.

Across core-collapse variants, the qualitative map holds: low-mass BH-BH ≈ stable mass transfer; narrow high-mass feature ≈ wind-driven non-interacting progenitors. Metallicity and winds decide where that feature sits, or whether it shows up at all.

07 · Conclusions

What to take home

Most Milky Way black holes are single today, but that usually means a binary story ended in a merger or a kick, not that the star was born alone. The binaries that stick around are mostly detached BH-BH systems with two solar-metallicity pathways baked in: stable mass transfer at lower masses, and a wind-driven ~13 M☉ plateau up high.

Natal kicks and common-envelope efficiency reshuffle who stays bound and how tight they are. Metallicity reshapes masses and can wipe out the wind fingerprint. Detailed binary evolution also turns up a stable-MT BH+star population that rapid codes underproduce, which is a nice place for Gaia to tell modeling approaches apart.

Next up in the paper's plan: forward-modeling. Take these intrinsic, kinematically evolved populations and ask what Gaia DR4 and Roman will actually detect.

Binary stars

Most of the stars you can pick out in the night sky are not loners. They are binaries or higher-order multiples — pairs (and triples, and more) locked in mutual orbits.

What “binary” means

A binary star is simply two stars bound by gravity, orbiting a shared center of mass. The components can be nearly twins or wildly unequal; the orbit can be days long or centuries long. Some pairs you can resolve in a telescope (visual binaries). Others only reveal themselves by a wobbling spectrum, eclipses, or a strange light curve.

Unequal-mass binary · shared center of mass

“Binary” is also shorthand for the whole family of multiple-star systems. Nature loves hierarchies: a close pair with a wider tertiary, or two binaries orbiting each other. For the physics that makes black holes, the important part is that stars grow up with neighbors that can steal mass, share envelopes, merge, or kick each other apart.

They are incredibly common

Among low-mass stars like the Sun, something like half have at least one companion. Climb the mass ladder and the binary fraction shoots up: for O- and early B-type stars — the ones that become black holes and neutron stars — most are born in binaries or multiples, with close companions especially common.

That is why the bright night sky feels so binary-rich. The stars our eyes notice are often luminous, and luminous stars are disproportionately the ones that formed with partners. Familiar names (Sirius, Alpha Centauri, Castor, and many more) are multiples. A lonely point of light is often a conspiracy of several suns.

Why this paper cares

Galactic black holes are the leftovers of massive stars. If those stars usually had partners, then mass transfer, common envelopes, mergers, and supernova kicks are not side plots — they are the plot. A “single” black hole today often still has a binary biography: a merger that made one star, or a kick that tore a pair apart.

POSYDON’s job is to follow those intertwined lives with detailed stellar models, then ask what the Galaxy’s quiet black holes should look like for Gaia and Roman. Without binaries, that story does not even start.
